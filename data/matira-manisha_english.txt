The illustrations of “Matira Manisha” are being presented here by the publishers. The renowned artist of Odisha, Shri Gopal Charan Kahnu, has drawn these pictures. Nowadays, on various new fabrics in the state, the style of S.L.H. rubber holds a special place. In this kind of sketch, there is not much abundance of color or detail. Just as, after listening to a good piece of music, a certain resonance lingers in the mind and leaves one enchanted for days, in the same way, a particular form or special impression leaves a subtle mark or trace in the mind of the artist. The inclusion of color and minute details of objects remains outside this essence. The S.L.H. style is merely an expression of this underlying impression. The illustrations of “Matira Manisha” are to be enjoyed in this way.

The originality of the artist lies in giving full expression to the final mood and events of the subject matter through these shadowy forms. The way he has interpreted “Matira Manisha” is his own. In Odisha, this is something quite new. I extend my congratulations to the artist.

Matira Manisha

Again, in the district of Cuttack, on the banks of the Sarupa river, lies the village of Padhanpada—a small, insignificant cluster of human dwellings. The house of Shama Pradhan, with its thatched roof and twenty-odd acres of land, stands above all in that village. Its place in the universe—who can say? Is it an atom or a molecule—who can tell?

Who was the first man to set foot on this earth, whose footprints remain here? Who was the first to till this land, to sow the first seeds, to taste its fruits? Neither the Barupa river nor the moon and sun can tell these tales. Yet, even today, after the paddy has been threshed, laughter rings out; even now, amidst the bustle of men, the village awakens. How many children have played on this ground—Shama Pradhan and his forefathers, and the elders before them! On the banks of the Barupa, the echoes of the villagers’ joys and sorrows still linger; in the village’s heart, the funeral processions of countless men have passed; in the early mornings, the cries of washerwomen lamenting their lost ones have mingled with the dew, and in the dry wind, their tears have become invisible—how long this has gone on, who can say!

There is no record of which ancestor of Shama Pradhan first settled in Padhanpada. How many have come and gone, how many kings and rulers—Hindus, Muslims, Marathas. Upon this earth, the glory of many religions has risen and faded—Hindu, Buddhist, Muslim, Christian! Yet no conqueror has ever claimed Padhanpada as his own. Like the tender blades of new grass that survive every storm, while mighty trees fall, no harm has ever come near it! Even the soil of Shama Pradhan’s land—

The Man of the Soil

In that village—no one’s courtyard was paved with stone; no one’s house had a stone threshold to keep out the mud; no one ever threw away four annas in idle gatherings, nor did any moneylender keep thousands of rupees at interest.

The dharmajaak of Padhaanpada has, since ancient times, been stationed at the feet of Mangala Maa, the goddess enshrined in that ancient stone temple. Whenever cholera or smallpox struck the village, it was to Mangala that everyone turned; whenever there was a wedding or the first rice of the season was cooked, it was Mangala who was invoked. If someone’s tree bore no fruit, if someone’s child fell ill, if a good man was seen, if new banana shoots sprouted in someone’s backyard, if the harvest was bountiful or poor—Mangala Maa always knew all the news of the village, she heard all the joys and sorrows, the good and bad of the people. For so many years, the vermilion on her forehead had never faded, nor had the cloth draped over her body ever slipped from her shoulders.

This is the old Padhaanpada, and this is its village—
So simple, so ordinary.

Among all the houses in the pada, the most well-known is the house of Shaama Padhaan—if not for its stone walls, then for the strength of its lineage!

Generation after generation, men have rebuilt that house. Once, when it was destroyed, it took ten years to restore—just a thatched hut with bamboo walls, like a village school. How many days could it withstand the rain and wind? I have seen Shaama Padhaan’s father and grandfather—men of sturdy build, strong as bulls! If the house ever collapsed, within a couple of days they would raise it up again from the mud.

Build a new house on the edge of the chak, Kamuda. Once you go to the chak, it’s a matter of twelve years—sometimes even ten years pass before you return. Even under the authority of Shyam Pradhan, I have done everything on my own land just like this. In the last decade, that house has been broken down three times, and three times it has been rebuilt anew.

For the sake of the old mother at home, for the sake of the men and cattle, Shyam Pradhan’s house is always visible from the edge of the village, near the akha of Pradhanpada. Whenever a yogi or a scripture-reader would come to the village, they would be shown Shyam Pradhan’s house. Whether there was a meeting in the village or outside, they would call for Shyam Pradhan; if someone was in distress or some calamity befell, they would call for Shyam Pradhan; if there was a wedding and the canopy had to be set up, they would call for Shyam Pradhan. Apart from knowing the Bhagavata from the village school, Shyam Pradhan did not know much else. He knew nothing of law, nothing of medicine, and he could not keep accounts like others.

Yet, in every matter, he was considered a little greater than his elder brother. If somewhere a midwife had to be called in the middle of the night, if a bamboo pole broke and had to be replaced, if the women needed to eat the ceremonial rice during a festival—Shyam Pradhan would be the one to get involved in all these things. It was not for any gain or profit. He never sought any reward in these matters. Oh, does he even get a handful of pithapana to eat in return? When there was any trouble or disaster in the village, they would call for Shyam Pradhan—then, as soon as he heard, he would run...

Sai says, —To whom should I speak? Has Shama ever listened? So many words are wasted for the sake of the house, but Pandab never hears them. It is all in vain.

There is a storm of quarrels raging in the village between two factions. Because of the land dispute, two or four houses will be ruined in the lawsuit. Shama, the village headman, will step in, plead, hold hands, even fall at their feet, and sit with tearful eyes from morning till afternoon — but as long as Shama is alive, let anyone do what they will, let them file lawsuits, let them quarrel — as long as he lives, this will not happen in the village.

“Why? Wait a few days and see. Our father and grandfather were men of the soil, they tilled Basudha’s land, did good deeds and passed away; as they left it, so it remains — not an inch of land has gone to strangers. As it was, so it will remain, passing from our sons to their sons. From this very courtyard, things will remain as they are. In the old granary, year after year, the earthen pots will be filled and emptied, the wooden doors will stand as they always have. If there is a flood, it will be swept clean — but not a speck of gold will be lost.”

In the village, he never uttered a word without meaning, nor did he speak just to fill his mouth. Though he was the son of a king, he only spoke what he understood to be right, and whatever he felt in his heart, he would say openly. Yet, once, he leaned his shoulder and gave a pillar to support his brother’s house. When his brother’s wife fell ill, he went around five villages to fetch medicine. For this, everyone respected his words; sometimes, even called him ‘Bedargar’ (the healer).

Let Parash say a few words, and if Shama Pradhan adds a few more, then the truth of the matter will come to light.

Whoever Shama Pradhan extended his hand to, became part of his family. He was a good man, a man of virtue. Even though she was a farmer’s daughter, what did it matter? Her heart was vast and generous. Whenever anyone in the neighboring village fell into distress, she would go straight to the Pradhan’s house. Why? The women of the village spoke of the Pradhan’s wife with deep respect. “The Pradhan’s old lady—she is truly blessed. Sebani says, “What of that? The year you had that terrible fever—when the cattle wandered off—that year onwards, the old woman has been limping about, barely able to move her arms and legs. If she ever goes to the pond, in two days she’ll be bedridden.”

The old woman worships the gods for days, fasts on every festival, and prays to be blessed with sons—Baraju and Chakada. Yet, she never calls either of her sons by name. Yes—those are men now; they must have names, but she never calls them by those. She calls the elder son ‘Shashukhia’, and the younger ‘Chagalpa’.

The old woman could see her sons as if they were right before her eyes, but she could never see her daughters-in-law in the same way. Born into such a lowly family—eating coarse rice, wearing rough cloth, enduring every hardship—what did they have to eat, what did they have to wear! For her, her life was for her husband, for her children—her happiness and sorrow were bound to theirs. What happiness could one seek in a woman’s life—what else was there to do?

The old woman’s heart sometimes broke with sorrow. What sin had she committed to be born into such a life, she could not guess! The daughters-in-law, too, sometimes bickered and quarreled—

Everyone is silent. The elder wife always scolds Sanboh more than anyone else. Sanboh never speaks back to her mother-in-law—I do. She listens to everything with downcast eyes, in silence.

Cows, oxen, and other animals cannot speak. Even dull-witted people cannot speak up. That is why people misunderstand them, they do not know what is in their hearts. For no reason at all, how much suffering they must bear, how often they are beaten unjustly, and yet they cannot protest or defend themselves. Sanboh’s condition is just like that. She listens to everything quietly, but she has no way to open her mouth. Sometimes, out of irritation, the old mother-in-law calls her a worm. After all, she is an old woman—how much can she understand the affairs of the house? Still, the house is hers—the daughter-in-law, for her, is the one who keeps the household together. Sometimes, in the depths of her old mind, the fear arises: what if the house falls apart because of her?
