One

One day, in the blazing midday of a Baishakh afternoon, the sun showed no mercy, and the heat knew no bounds. At that very hour, Devdas, son of the Mukherjees, sat in a corner of the schoolroom upon a torn mat, slate in hand, gazing, blinking, stretching his legs, yawning—and at last, quite suddenly, became deeply pensive. In a moment, he resolved that to be confined within the schoolhouse at such a glorious hour, when one might be flying kites across the fields, was nothing short of a calamity. His fertile mind quickly devised a plan. Slate in hand, he stood up.

It was now tiffin break at the school. The boys, in various attitudes and with much noise, were playing dang-guli beneath the nearby banyan tree. Devdas glanced that way. He did not receive a tiffin break—for Govinda Pandit had often observed that once Devdas left the schoolhouse, he was most reluctant to return. His father, too, had forbidden it. For various reasons, it had been decided that, during this time, he would remain in the custody of Sardaar-Poro Bhulo.

Now, within the room, only Pandit Mahashoy lay dozing in the torpor of the afternoon, his eyes closed, while Sardaar-Poro Bhulo, perched in a corner upon a broken bench, sat pretending to be a little pandit himself, and, from time to time, watched the game with utter disdain, or cast lazy, mocking glances at Devdas and Parvati. Parvati had come under Pandit Mahashoy’s care and supervision only a month ago. Perhaps, in this short span, the Pandit had managed to thoroughly amuse her, for she now sat absorbed, with infinite patience, copying the likeness of the sleeping Pandit onto the last page of her primer, and, like a skilled artist, studied in various ways how closely her careful drawing matched the original. The resemblance was not great, yet Parvati found in it much joy and self-satisfaction.

At this moment, Devdas stood up, slate in hand, and called out to Bhulo, “I cannot do the sums.”

At that very moment, an incident occurred. For the past three years, Sardar-poro had been selecting the broken, rickety bench as his rightful seat, befitting his status, and sitting there every day as was his custom. Behind him lay a heap of lime, which the learned Pandit Mahashaya had once bought at a bargain price in some bygone era, with the intention that, when fortune smiled, he would use it to build a house or a hall. Who knows when that auspicious day would come, but his care and vigilance over this white powder knew no bounds. No inexperienced, thoughtless, ill-fated child could be allowed to waste even a speck of it; thus, his beloved and comparatively older Bholanath had been entrusted with the careful guardianship of this treasured store, and he would sit on the bench, guarding it diligently.

Bholanath was writing, “If the price of one maund of oil is fourteen rupees, nine annas, and three gandas, then—oh, father, oh!”—when suddenly a great commotion arose. Parvati, in a shrill voice, clapped her hands and fell to the ground, rolling in laughter. Govinda Pandit, just awakened from sleep, stood up abruptly, his eyes bloodshot; he saw that under the tree, the boys were running in a row, shouting and making a racket, and at that very moment he noticed a pair of feet dancing atop the broken bench, while the lime heap erupted like a volcano. He shouted, “What—what—what is happening?”

Of those present, only Parvati could have answered. But she was rolling on the ground, clapping her hands. The Pandit Mahashaya’s futile question returned to him in anger, “What, what—what is happening?”

Three  
Day after day passed—there was no end to the amusements of these two children. All day long they wandered about in the blazing sun, returning at dusk only to be scolded and beaten, and then, come morning, they would run off and escape again—only to face rebuke and punishment once more.  
At night, they slept peacefully, free from care; morning would come, and again they would run away to play. They had no other close companions, nor did they need any. The two of them, together, were enough to cause mischief throughout the neighborhood.  
That day, just after sunrise, the two of them had gone down to the pond. By midday, their eyes were red, the water thoroughly muddied, and they had caught fifteen small fish, which they divided between themselves as they saw fit.

It is the nature of cautious and experienced men that they do not, in the blink of an eye, pronounce judgment on the merits or faults of any matter. They do not form an opinion without weighing everything; they do not speak of all sides after seeing only two. But there is another kind of man, quite the opposite! Such men lack the patience to ponder anything for long. The moment something falls into their hands, they decide—this is good, or this is bad—without the labor of careful consideration, relying instead on the strength of their own convictions. * Now, after all this time, I do not know what has become of Parvati, or how she fares. I do not even wish to inquire. Only for Devdas does my heart ache deeply. Whoever among you reads this tale may perhaps feel sorrow as we did. Yet, if ever you come to know someone as ill-fated, as weak and sinful as Devdas, offer a prayer for him. Pray that, whatever else may befall, no one’s end comes as his did. There is no harm in dying, but at that final moment, may a gentle, affectionate touch reach his brow—may the last sight of this life be a face full of tender compassion. At the hour of death, may he see a single tear in someone’s eye, and thus depart.
